Evidence

Not every finding is equally strong. We tell you which is which.

Health statements on Nurtre carry one of four labels, from firm clinical guidance to early ideas. Here’s what each label means, how we choose it, and every source we rely on.

The four labels

From strongest to earliest

Each label has its own marker shape, so you can tell them apart without relying on color.

Strongest Earliest
  1. Established guidance Filled circle

    What it means

    Reflected in major clinical practice guidelines and supported by consistent evidence. Expert panels have reviewed the research and agree.

    How to read it

    A safe foundation to act on, together with your care team.

    Example on Nurtre

    “Fiber slows digestion, so the sugar from a meal enters your blood more gradually and you stay full longer.”

    Foods: Why fiber matters
  2. Research finding Half-filled circle

    What it means

    Seen in controlled studies, such as randomized trials or meta-analyses, but not yet strong or consistent enough to be standard guidance.

    How to read it

    Promising and worth discussing, but results may be small, mixed, or measured on lab values rather than outcomes you’d notice.

    Example on Nurtre

    “Better lab numbers don’t always mean a better chance of having a baby.”

    Supplements: Trying to conceive
  3. Association Hollow circle

    What it means

    A link observed in population or observational studies. Two things tend to go together, but that doesn’t show that one causes the other.

    How to read it

    Useful for spotting patterns. Other factors could explain the link.

    Example on Nurtre

    “Studies have found that people with PCOS tend to eat less fiber, and lower intake has been linked with higher insulin resistance.”

    Foods: Why fiber matters
  4. Emerging hypothesis Dashed circle

    What it means

    An early idea based on limited or preliminary research, such as small studies, lab work, or a plausible mechanism.

    How to read it

    Worth watching, not acting on. Many early ideas don’t hold up in larger studies.

    Example on Nurtre

    “Cinnamon supplements are being studied for blood sugar in PCOS, but amounts in food are much smaller.”

    Foods: Cinnamon

How we choose a label

The type of evidence decides the label

We start from the strongest kind of evidence available for a statement and work down.

  1. 1

    Is it in a major clinical guideline?

    For example, the 2023 International PCOS Guideline or World Health Organization guidance.

    Established guidance
  2. 2

    Do controlled trials or systematic reviews support it?

    Studies that compare a treatment with a control group, or reviews that pool many of them.

    Research finding
  3. 3

    Is it a pattern seen in observational studies?

    Studies that observe groups of people without changing what they do.

    Association
  4. 4

    Is it based on early or limited work?

    Small studies, lab or animal research, or a mechanism that makes sense but hasn’t been tested well.

    Emerging hypothesis
  • 🏷️

    We label the statement, not the source

    A strong review can still include weak findings. Each claim gets the label its own evidence supports.

  • ⚖️

    Mixed evidence gets the lower label

    When studies disagree, we choose the more cautious label rather than the most exciting result.

  • 🎯

    Outcomes matter more than lab values

    A change in a blood test isn’t the same as feeling better or having a baby. We say which one a study measured.

  • 📏

    Nutrition numbers aren’t labeled

    Values like grams of fiber are measurements from USDA data, not claims, so they don’t carry an evidence label.

Limits

What our labels can’t do

  • 🩺

    They aren’t medical advice

    Evidence describes groups of people. Your doctor or dietitian can tell you what applies to you.

  • 🔄

    Evidence changes

    New studies can move a statement up or down. Each page shows when it was last updated.

  • 🧭

    Labels involve judgment

    We follow the steps above, but deciding where a finding fits is still a judgment call, and experts can disagree.

  • 🔍

    We check every citation

    Each source below was checked against its publisher, CrossRef, or PubMed in September 2026, and links to the original.
