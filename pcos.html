Definition

What is PCOS?

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common, long-term health condition that affects about 1 in 8 women of reproductive age. It is a complex condition involving both hormonal and metabolic imbalances, and for many people the metabolic side, especially insulin resistance, plays a central role.

PCOS can cause a wide variety of symptoms, including irregular periods, weight changes, excess hair growth, acne, and oily skin. It is also linked to a higher risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease. Symptoms look different from person to person, and PCOS occurs in people of all body sizes.

PCOS is also one of the most common causes of ovulation-related infertility, because it can disrupt ovulation: the ovaries don't regularly mature and release an egg.

A name that's evolving

The name Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) has been proposed to better reflect the metabolic effects that are an important part of the condition. For now, PCOS remains the established name.

Not really cysts

Despite the name, PCOS doesn't involve true cysts. What show up on an ultrasound are small follicles: fluid-filled sacs holding eggs that stalled before they were mature enough to be released.

The metabolic core

Insulin is at the heart of PCOS

Insulin is a hormone that helps move glucose from your blood into your cells for energy. In many people with PCOS, the body becomes less responsive to insulin, a change called insulin resistance. That one change can ripple through the whole body.

  1. 1

    Cells respond less to insulin

    Glucose has a harder time getting into cells, so more of it stays in the blood.

    Established guidance
  2. 2

    The body makes more insulin

    To compensate, the pancreas releases extra insulin to keep blood sugar in range.

    Established guidance
  3. 3

    The ovaries make more androgens

    High insulin can signal the ovaries to produce more androgens and lower SHBG, a protein that normally keeps them in check.

    Research finding
  4. 4

    Ovulation and symptoms are affected

    Higher androgens disrupt follicle development and ovulation, contributing to irregular periods, acne, and excess hair growth.

    Research finding

It can become a cycle. Higher androgen levels may, in turn, worsen insulin resistance.

Whole-body effects

How PCOS shows up in the body

PCOS affects several systems at once. Not everyone has every feature, and symptoms can change over time.

  • Often at the center

    Metabolic

    How your body handles insulin and blood sugar. It influences hormones, weight, and long-term health, which is why it's a major focus throughout Nurtre.

    • Insulin resistance
    • Weight gain or difficulty managing weight, often around the abdomen
    • Higher blood sugar, cholesterol, or blood pressure over time

  • Hormonal

    Higher levels of androgens, hormones present in everyone but usually lower in women.

    • Elevated androgens, such as testosterone
    • Imbalance in the hormones that coordinate the cycle

  • Reproductive

    Changes to the menstrual cycle and ovulation.

    • Irregular, infrequent, or missed periods
    • Irregular or absent ovulation
    • Difficulty getting pregnant

  • Skin & hair

    Visible signs often linked to higher androgens.

    • Excess hair growth on the face, chest, or back
    • Acne and oily skin
    • Thinning hair on the scalp

  • Emotional wellbeing

    PCOS can affect how you feel, not just your body.

    • Higher rates of anxiety and depression
    • Body image concerns
    • Stress around symptoms or fertility

Long-term health

Why metabolic health matters over time

Because PCOS is a metabolic condition, its effects reach beyond periods and fertility. Knowing the risks helps you and your healthcare team plan ahead, and many of them can be screened for and managed.

  • Type 2 diabetes

    Insulin resistance raises the chance of developing type 2 diabetes, so regular blood sugar checks are recommended.

    Established guidance

  • Gestational diabetes

    A higher chance of developing diabetes during pregnancy.

    Established guidance

  • Cholesterol & blood pressure

    Less favorable cholesterol levels and higher blood pressure are more common, so these are worth checking.

    Established guidance

  • Heart disease

    PCOS is linked with more heart disease risk factors. Whether it directly raises rates of heart attack and stroke is still being studied.

    Association

  • Fatty liver disease

    Metabolic fatty liver disease is seen more often in people with PCOS.

    Association

Diagnosis

How PCOS is diagnosed

In adults, PCOS is usually diagnosed when at least two of these three features are present, after other conditions with similar symptoms have been ruled out.

Established guidance
  1. 1

    Irregular cycles

    Periods that are infrequent, irregular, or absent, a sign that ovulation isn't happening regularly.

  2. 2

    Higher androgens

    Seen through signs like excess hair growth or acne, or measured with a blood test.

  3. 3

    Ovary findings

    Many small follicles on an ultrasound, or a high anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) level in adults.

Different for teens

In adolescents, diagnosis relies on irregular cycles and higher androgens together. Ultrasound isn't recommended within 8 years of a first period, because many follicles are normal at that age.

Recognize these signs?

Only a healthcare professional can diagnose PCOS. If these features sound familiar, a doctor can run the right checks and talk through next steps with you.

Where nutrition fits in

Everyday eating is one of your most practical tools

Because insulin plays such a central role in PCOS, healthy eating and activity habits are recommended for everyone with the condition. There's no single best "PCOS diet". Consistent, sustainable habits matter more than any one plan.
